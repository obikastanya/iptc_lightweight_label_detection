\begin{abstract}
Filtering news by the International Press Telecommunications Council (IPTC) Media Topic taxonomy needs a classifier that is cheap to run on a multilingual stream of articles. The open multilingual IPTC classifier, based on XLM-RoBERTa-large, takes 1,239 to 2,971 milliseconds per article on one processor core. It is distilled into StaticKD, a student built on multilingual static embeddings whose linear head is folded into the embedding table, so the whole model is one table of 17 logits per token. The teacher labels 285,999 unlabeled articles in 69 languages. A test set of 12,744 articles in 47 languages, labeled by human annotators or by the sections that newsroom editors assigned and mapped onto the IPTC tree, allows evaluation without labels from any model. On this test set StaticKD reaches a macro-F1 of 0.545, or 91.1\% of the teacher score of 0.598. It classifies 473 articles per second per core, about 616 times faster than the 8-bit quantized teacher, with a 34-megabyte model, and it is more accurate than fastText and a linear support vector machine distilled on the same data. Ablations with five seeds per variant show that accuracy comes from multilingual distillation data and cross-lingually aligned embeddings, while hard labels match soft labels. Transfer to languages absent from the distillation data is carried by embedding rows that training never updates, and fine-tuning the embeddings trades this transfer for in-distribution fit. StaticKD is weakest on languages missing from both the distillation data and the pre-training data of the teacher.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{N}{ews} analytics pipelines assign articles to standard topics early, so that the more expensive stages only process the topics that matter. The taxonomy most used by news agencies is Media Topic of the International Press Telecommunications Council (IPTC). Its top level has 17 topics, such as \emph{politics}, \emph{economy, business and finance}, and \emph{sport}~\cite{iptc2024mediatopics,kuzman2025llm}.

Kuzman and Ljube\v{s}i\'c~\cite{kuzman2025llm} released the first open multilingual IPTC classifier. They labeled articles in four languages with GPT-4o and used these labels to fine-tune XLM-RoBERTa-large~\cite{conneau2020xlmr}. The resulting model matches GPT-4o on a human-annotated test set. A follow-up study by the same group concluded that fine-tuned encoders are more practical than large language models (LLMs) for large-scale annotation~\cite{kuzman2025southslavic}. An encoder with 559.9~million parameters is still expensive without a graphics processing unit (GPU). On one central processing unit (CPU) core the model needs 2,971~ms per article, and its fastest CPU configuration, an Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) export quantized to 8-bit integers (int8), still needs 1,239~ms (Section~\ref{sec:res-eff}). Neither study reports this inference cost, although inference accounts for a large share of the computing demand and energy use of machine learning in production, for example 60\% of the machine learning energy use reported by Google~\cite{luccioni2024power}.

The target is a topic classifier that covers many languages, runs in milliseconds per article on an ordinary CPU, and has a small footprint and few dependencies. Existing options fall short of this. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and other parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods~\cite{hu2022lora,razuvayevskaya2024peft} reduce the cost of training, not of inference, because the merged model keeps the size of its backbone. Small distilled transformers~\cite{sanh2019distilbert,wang2021minilmv2} gain about one order of magnitude. In the CPU benchmark of this study, MiniLM-L6 is 21 times faster than the ONNX int8 teacher. Int8 quantization alone gives a factor of 2.3 over PyTorch fp32. fastText~\cite{joulin2017bag} is much faster, but it learns its embeddings from scratch and therefore does not transfer across languages. This matters here because labeled IPTC data exists for only four languages.

This paper proposes StaticKD, which distills~\cite{hinton2015distilling} the XLM-R-large IPTC classifier into a student that, after training, is a single table of 17 logits per token. A teacher that works across languages can label unlabeled news in any language at no annotation cost. Multilingual static embeddings distilled from sentence encoders~\cite{tulkens2024model2vec,wada2025static,chen2024bgem3} place translation equivalents close to each other, and looking them up costs almost nothing. News topics are also strongly lexical, which keeps bag-of-words representations competitive for topic classification~\cite{galke2022bow}. Because the classification head of StaticKD is linear, the averaged embedding and the head can be folded into a token table. Inference then reduces to tokenization and averaging table rows, which NumPy handles.

A multilingual claim requires a multilingual evaluation with labels that do not come from the teacher. The human test set of Kuzman and Ljube\v{s}i\'c is not public and covers four languages. SIB-200~\cite{adelani2024sib200} covers 205 languages, but its items are short sentences with a non-IPTC taxonomy. To fill this gap, a unified test set of 12,744 articles in 47 languages is built. It combines CC-NEWS articles from 2024 to 2026 that carry their newsroom section in schema.org metadata~\cite{guha2016schema} with public datasets labeled by humans or by publisher categories~\cite{adelani2023masakhanews,petukhova2023mnds,mirashi2023indicnews,hossain2025banglanews}. The labels are editorial decisions of publishers or annotators, translated onto the official IPTC tree. No model that reads the article text is involved.

This study addresses four research questions (RQs).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (cost):} What is the CPU inference cost of the IPTC teacher, and how much does StaticKD reduce it?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (accuracy):} How much of the teacher accuracy does StaticKD retain on labels that do not come from the teacher, compared with baselines of similar cost, namely fastText and a support vector machine (SVM) on term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) features, and with baselines of higher cost, namely MiniLM-L6 and DistilmBERT?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (components):} Which distillation components determine the accuracy of this very-low-capacity student: soft labels, multilingual data, embedding initialization, embedding fine-tuning, token dropout, temperature, or ensembling?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (generalization):} On which languages, labels, and label sources is StaticKD weak, and which factors explain it?
\end{itemize}

Distilling into a student of such low capacity also shows where its accuracy comes from. The knowledge that matters comes from the coverage of the transfer set and from the aligned initialization, not from the shape of the soft labels. Hard labels match soft labels, and a high temperature hurts. Transfer to languages that never appear in the distillation data is carried by embedding rows that training never updates, which works because they share a space with the trained rows. Fine-tuning the embeddings trades this transfer for in-distribution fit, a representation drift that a development set in the training languages cannot detect.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A multilingual IPTC test set of 12,744 articles in 47 languages whose editorial labels do not come from the teacher. It comes with a section-to-IPTC mapping table that is validated automatically and reviewed by the first author, cross-source deduplication, a contamination check against all training data, and site-cluster bootstrap confidence intervals, and it can be reused to evaluate other IPTC classifiers.
  \item The findings above, supported by ablations with five seeds per variant and by analyses by language, label, label origin, and source, including languages that are absent from the distillation data.
  \item StaticKD, a recipe that distills a multilingual transformer teacher into a token-logit table initialized from cross-lingual static embeddings. The final model takes 34~MB, needs only \texttt{numpy} and \texttt{tokenizers}, and processes 473 articles per second per core. An ensemble of seeds folds into the same table at no extra inference cost. Its accuracy--cost trade-off is compared with nine configurations from six model families, measured on CPU only with core pinning for a hybrid CPU.
  \item A release of the code, a notebook that reproduces every number in this paper, the models, and the test-set manifest.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Multilingual News Topic Classification and the IPTC Scheme}
Resources for news topic classification usually define their own taxonomy and cover a single language or region. MasakhaNEWS covers 16 languages widely spoken in Africa, English and French included, with seven human-annotated categories~\cite{adelani2023masakhanews}. L3Cube-IndicNews uses publisher site categories for 10 Indian languages~\cite{mirashi2023indicnews}, and a Bangla news dataset also relies on publisher categories~\cite{hossain2025banglanews}. MN-DS is the only public dataset annotated directly with top-level IPTC topics, and it is English only~\cite{petukhova2023mnds}. SIB-200 spans 205 languages, but its items are Flores-200 sentences with seven non-IPTC topics~\cite{adelani2024sib200}. Kuzman and Ljube\v{s}i\'c~\cite{kuzman2025llm} showed that GPT-4o labels can replace human annotation for training an IPTC classifier, and their follow-up work found fine-tuned encoders more practical than LLMs for large-scale annotation~\cite{kuzman2025southslavic}. As a result, the only open multilingual IPTC classifier is an encoder with 559.9~million parameters, and no inexpensive IPTC classifier has been evaluated on real news in many languages.

\subsection{Distilling Transformer Classifiers into Compact Students}
Knowledge distillation (KD) trains a cheap model to imitate the temperature-softened output distribution of an expensive one~\cite{hinton2015distilling}. A recent survey covers its development for language models~\cite{yang2025kdsurvey}. In text classification the student is almost always a transformer, for example DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} or MiniLMv2~\cite{wang2021minilmv2}, including cross-lingual distillation that moves teacher knowledge to languages with little data~\cite{piperno2025crosslingual}. The closest prior work is Sparse Distillation~\cite{ye2022sparse}, which distills RoBERTa-large into a deep averaging network~\cite{iyyer2015dan} with a very large n-gram table and runs hundreds of times faster on English single-sentence classification tasks. StaticKD differs in three ways. Its table starts from multilingual static embeddings that are already aligned across languages instead of being trained from scratch. Its linear head is folded so that each token stores only 17 numbers. It targets news documents in 69 languages and is evaluated on languages that are missing from the distillation data. PEFT methods such as LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora,razuvayevskaya2024peft} do not belong to this group, because their inference cost stays that of the backbone.

\subsection{Temperature and the Capacity Gap}
How well distillation works depends on the capacity gap between teacher and student. For language models, the optimal teacher size even follows an empirical law with respect to the student size~\cite{zhang2025law}. Temperature is treated as an important hyperparameter that can be learned~\cite{li2023ctkd}. The Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence can be split into a target-class part and a non-target part (dark knowledge)~\cite{zhao2022dkd}, and recent analysis shows that KL under a capacity mismatch distorts the ranking of classes~\cite{wang2026rckd}. Fidelity to the teacher also does not always go together with generalization~\cite{stanton2021kd}. Nearly all of these findings come from deep students. How temperature and soft labels behave at an extreme gap, from 559.9~million parameters to one linear layer over averaged embeddings, has not been characterized.

\subsection{Static Embeddings and Efficient CPU Inference}
Model2Vec~\cite{tulkens2024model2vec} and Wada et al.~\cite{wada2025static} extract static word embeddings from sentence transformers, and averaging them yields competitive sentence embeddings. The potion-multilingual variant is distilled from BGE-M3~\cite{chen2024bgem3} for 101 languages. The Model2Vec library also includes a trainer that turns such a model into a classifier. It fine-tunes the embeddings and a weight per token, adds a hidden layer of 512 units, and trains with cross-entropy on hard labels~\cite{tulkens2024model2vec}. Such embeddings are usually evaluated as general-purpose embeddings, not as distillation students for a specific taxonomy. Simple bag-of-words representations remain competitive for topic classification~\cite{galke2022bow}. A survey of efficient natural language processing (NLP) methods recommends reporting inference cost next to accuracy~\cite{treviso2023efficient}, since inference makes up a large share of the energy use of deployed models~\cite{luccioni2024power}. Few studies, however, report CPU latency at low thread counts against a quantized teacher.

\subsection{Test Sets with Editorial Labels, Contamination, and Sample Dependence}
Publisher site categories have been used as topic labels before~\cite{mirashi2023indicnews}, usually for one publisher or one region. The schema.org \texttt{articleSection} metadata~\cite{guha2016schema} has not yet been mapped to a standard taxonomy across dozens of languages. Leakage between training and test data also has to be controlled, and near-duplicate removal with MinHash~\cite{broder1997resemblance} has become standard practice~\cite{lee2022dedup}. Significance tests in NLP such as the paired bootstrap~\cite{koehn2004significance} usually assume independent samples~\cite{dror2018hitchhiker}, while articles from the same site are dependent. The cluster bootstrap~\cite{field2007bootstrapping} handles this dependence by resampling clusters instead of documents. Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes how StaticKD relates to these approaches.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Study Design}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the study. The teacher labels a distillation corpus, on which StaticKD and all baselines are trained. A separate test set, whose labels do not come from the teacher, serves for the accuracy evaluation with statistical tests and for the subgroup analyses, and inference cost is measured on CPU. The StaticKD recipe was fixed on the development set \emph{before} the test set was built, and no hyperparameter was selected on the test set. During this exploration, a small check set of 320 CC-News articles in 16 languages, labeled by Claude with the prompt and label descriptions of~\cite{kuzman2025llm}, also served as a secondary signal next to the development set and the held-out agreement. It accompanied the choice of $\tau=1$ and of the three-seed table ensemble, formed from the three seeds run in that phase. LLM labels are too close to the teacher setting to serve as an independent test, so this check set was dropped from the final evaluation.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Given a news article $d$ in any language, consisting of its title and body truncated to the first 512 words as in~\cite{kuzman2025llm}, a model predicts one label $y\in\mathcal{Y}$ among the 17 top-level IPTC topics. The teacher $f_T$ outputs logits $\mathbf{z}^T(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$. The goal is a student $f_S$ whose inference costs $O(n)$ additions for $n$ tokens, without matrix multiplication and without a deep learning library, and whose accuracy against independent labels is as close as possible to that of $f_T$.

\subsection{Distillation Data and Development Set}
EMMediaTopic 1.0~\cite{emmediatopic2025,kuzman2025llm} contains Catalan, Greek, Croatian, and Slovenian articles labeled by GPT-4o. Its training split (20,000 articles) is part of the distillation data. Its development split (1,000 articles, median 218 words) was never used to train the teacher or the student and serves as the development set for choosing the recipe.

The multilingual CC-News corpus~\cite{cloversearch_ccnews} from the 2019--2021 archive was processed with the following steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Keep only documents whose metadata language agrees with fastText language identification~\cite{joulin2017bag}.
  \item Take the title and body with at least 75 words (200 characters for ja, zh, th, my) and truncate to 512 words.
  \item Keep at most 150 documents per site.
  \item Form a held-out split by domain: all documents of some sites are set aside, at most 20\% per language.
  \item Add 156,157 documents from training sites only (at most 5,000 per language and 400 per site).
\end{enumerate}
The distillation data contain 285,999 documents in 69 languages from 2,960 sites, with a median of 269 words. The held-out split is domain-disjoint within each language. A few multilingual sites (for example bbc.com) also appear in other languages of the distillation data, so their articles were removed. The remaining fidelity set has 12,554 documents in 63 languages from 217 sites that never occur in the distillation data.

\subsection{Unified Test Set Construction}
\label{sec:testset}
The first source is CC-NEWS 2024--2026, sampled as one WARC file per month from January 2024 to August 2026 (32 files) of the Common Crawl CC-NEWS archive~\cite{commoncrawl2026ccnews}. From each page, the schema.org JSON-LD blocks~\cite{guha2016schema} of type \texttt{NewsArticle} and its subtypes were extracted, with the fields \texttt{articleSection}, \texttt{BreadcrumbList}, \texttt{inLanguage}, \texttt{datePublished}, \texttt{isAccessibleForFree}, and \texttt{articleBody}. The text comes from \texttt{articleBody} when it has at least 30 words and is otherwise extracted with Trafilatura~\cite{barbaresi2021trafilatura}. Sites that occur in the distillation data or in another evaluation set were removed, which leaves 399,468 articles from new sites.

The second source is public datasets. MasakhaNEWS~\cite{adelani2023masakhanews} and MN-DS~\cite{petukhova2023mnds} are labeled by humans. L3Cube-IndicNews (LDC split)~\cite{mirashi2023indicnews}, Bangla news~\cite{hossain2025banglanews}, IndoNews~\cite{indonews2021}, Amharic news~\cite{amharicnews2024}, and Swahili news~\cite{swahilinews2020} carry publisher categories. The last two were processed in the same way but were not selected during sampling, because MasakhaNEWS already filled their language cells with human labels. The second source holds 312,551 articles in total.

Section and category labels represent \emph{editorial topics}, that is, the decision of the newsroom about where an article belongs when it is published. These labels come from no model that reads the article text, so they are not circular with respect to the teacher. The only added step is translating section names onto the official IPTC Media Topic tree (1,085 active nodes). Section names are normalized (case, diacritics) and mapped conservatively. A name is mapped only if the content usually found under that section belongs to one top-level topic. This gives 814 names mapped to 79 nodes. The table deliberately leaves 62 ambiguous names unmapped, for example \emph{tech}, which can be \emph{science and technology} or the information technology industry under \emph{economy}. Likewise, \emph{entertainment} can be \emph{arts} or \emph{lifestyle}, and \emph{terrorism} can be \emph{conflict} or \emph{crime}. Astrology sections are rejected. An automatic check verifies that every target node exists in the IPTC tree, is not retired, and has the target label as its level-1 ancestor. Articles whose sections map to different labels are rejected. MN-DS needs no mapping, because its labels already are IPTC topics. The mapping table was drafted with artificial intelligence assistance, and the first author reviewed all of its 916 rules (814 mapped names and 102 ambiguous, language-specific, and rejecting rules) and accepted them without changes.

Quality and leakage filters are applied in order.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Publication date on or after 1 January 2024 (CC-NEWS only).
  \item Not behind a paywall.
  \item Declared language agrees with fastText language identification when the language is supported.
  \item At least 75 words.
  \item Deduplication of uniform resource locators (URLs), identical texts, and near-duplicates (MinHash~\cite{broder1997resemblance}, Jaccard $\ge 0.7$~\cite{lee2022dedup}) across all sources.
  \item Contamination: an article is dropped when $\ge 30\%$ of its word 8-grams occur in any of about 300 thousand training or evaluation documents. This filter removed 50 articles, mostly from MasakhaNEWS, which also draws on BBC.
\end{enumerate}

Sampling takes at most 50 articles per language $\times$ label cell, preferring human-labeled sources, then publisher categories, then JSON-LD sections. Languages with fewer than 30 articles are dropped. The number of articles per site is not capped, because most cells come from few sites and such a cap would discard data without adding diversity. The evaluation handles the dependence between articles of the same site with a site-cluster bootstrap, and Section~\ref{sec:res-acc} reports a sensitivity test with at most five articles per site.

The final test set contains 12,744 articles in 47 languages, with 17 labels and 1,227 sites or sources and a median of 313 words. Of these, 4,094 articles (32.1\%) carry human annotations, 2,600 (20.4\%) publisher categories, and 6,050 (47.5\%) JSON-LD sections. By the distillation data in their language, 13 languages are entirely absent from the distillation data (for example ha, yo, ig, bn, pa), 9 have fewer than 500 documents, and 25 have more. By site diversity, 13 languages have at least 10 effective sites, while 27 languages come from practically one publisher. Table~\ref{tab:testset} summarizes the sources and Fig.~\ref{fig:testset} shows the language $\times$ label coverage.

\subsection{Teacher and Distillation Labels}
The teacher is the IPTC classifier of~\cite{kuzman2025llm}, published as \url{classla/multilingual-IPTC-news-topic-classifier}. It is an XLM-RoBERTa-large model with 24 layers, 559.9 million parameters, and inputs of at most 512 tokens. Its architecture limits the input to 512 XLM-R tokens. The 512-word input of 60.7\% of the test articles exceeds this limit, and for those articles the teacher reads about 287 of 438 words (medians). For every distillation document the teacher produces a logit vector $\mathbf{z}^{T}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{17}$, which is stored in full. Labeling ran once in fp16 on an NVIDIA RTX~4050 Laptop GPU (6~GB), with batches sorted by text length. On a fixed sample of 5,000 documents the teacher labels 50.1 documents per second, so the whole distillation set takes about 1.58 GPU hours. The arg-max predictions of this re-run agree with the stored labels for 100\% of the documents. The teacher label distribution on CC-News is plausible (\emph{politics} 16.5\%, \emph{economy} 12.7\%, \emph{sport} 11.6\%, \emph{weather} 1.3\%), and its mean confidence per language ranges from 0.90 to 1.00.

\subsection{The StaticKD Student}
A document $d$ is tokenized into $t_1,\dots,t_n$ with the potion-multilingual-128M Unigram tokenizer~\cite{tulkens2024model2vec}, which has 500,353 tokens. Special tokens are not used, unknown tokens are dropped, and the length is capped at 1,024 tokens. Averaging has no position limit, so the cap only bounds the cost. Only 5.4\% of the test articles exceed it. Each token has an embedding $\mathbf{e}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$, and
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}(d)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{e}_{t_i},\qquad
\mathbf{z}^{S}(d)=W\,\mathrm{drop}(\mathbf{h}(d))+\mathbf{b},
\label{eq:student}
\end{equation}
with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{17\times 256}$ and dropout 0.1 during training. The embedding matrix $E$ is initialized from potion-multilingual, static embeddings for 101 languages distilled from BGE-M3~\cite{chen2024bgem3}.

The loss in \eqref{eq:kd} uses $p^{T}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}^{T}/\tau)$ and $p^{S}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}^{S}/\tau)$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}=\tau^{2}\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p^{T}\,\middle\|\,p^{S}\right),\qquad \tau=1 .
\label{eq:kd}
\end{equation}
$E$, $W$, and $\mathbf{b}$ are trained end to end. $E$ uses SparseAdam ($\eta=10^{-3}$), so only the rows of tokens present in a batch are updated, while $W$ and $\mathbf{b}$ use AdamW ($\eta=3\cdot 10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$). The batch size is 64 and training runs for 6 epochs. As a cheap augmentation, each token of a training document is dropped with probability 0.2 at every step (token dropout), so that the model does not rely on a few keywords. Internal validation uses 3\% of the distillation data (8,579 documents), and the epoch with the highest arg-max agreement with the teacher is kept.

Because the classification head is linear, \eqref{eq:student} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
W\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\mathbf{e}_{t_i}\Big)+\mathbf{b}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}T_{t_i}+\mathbf{b},
\label{eq:fold}
\end{equation}
with the table $T=EW^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times 17}$. The production model consists only of $T$ (fp16), $\mathbf{b}$, and the tokenizer, and inference is tokenization, averaging table rows, and adding the bias. Averaging the logits of $K$ students trained with different seeds is likewise equal to the logits of a single student with table $\bar{T}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_k T^{(k)}$ and bias $\bar{\mathbf{b}}$, because \eqref{eq:fold} is linear in $T$. The final StaticKD model is the table ensemble of seeds 0, 1, and 2 ($K=3$), with 8.5 million parameters and a size of 34~MB.

The internal structure of the Unigram tokenizer needs much more random-access memory (RAM) than the table itself (16~MB). A compact variant shrinks that structure by removing token pieces that occur fewer than 10 times in the distillation corpus, keeping single characters. Its vocabulary has 335,441 tokens and the model takes 23~MB. Table~\ref{tab:hparams} lists the hyperparameters of all models.

\subsection{Baselines}
All baselines are trained on the same distillation data (285,999 documents) with targets from the same teacher. fastText + KD~\cite{joulin2017bag} is trained on teacher arg-max labels with word tokens (plus character n-grams) or XLM-R subwords, together with a quantized variant (\texttt{.ftz}). As a reference equivalent to the labeled data available to~\cite{kuzman2025llm}, fastText is also trained only on the GPT-4o labels of the EMMediaTopic training split. TF-IDF + SVM + KD is the non-neural baseline of Kuzman and Ljube\v{s}i\'c (TF-IDF features with a support vector classifier), trained on teacher arg-max labels over XLM-R subwords. The hyperparameters of these non-transformer baselines were set once, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:hparams}, without a search. MiniLM-L6 + KD uses mMiniLMv2-L6-H384, distilled from XLM-R-large~\cite{wang2021minilmv2}, with the temperature $\tau\in\{1,2\}$ chosen on the development set. DistilmBERT + KD uses \texttt{distilbert-base-multilingual-cased}~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} with the same recipe. Both transformers read at most 256 tokens, which truncates 91.1\% (MiniLM-L6) and 93.2\% (DistilmBERT) of the test articles to about 142 and 116 words. Both are exported to ONNX fp32 for the CPU benchmark. The ONNX int8 teacher is a dynamic int8 quantized export of the teacher and is its fastest CPU configuration.

Sparse Distillation~\cite{ye2022sparse}, the closest prior work, was not reproduced in full. Two experiments of this study cover its core ideas. fastText + KD is a bag-of-n-grams model with averaging and a linear head, trained on teacher labels over the same transfer set. The random-initialization ablation (Section~\ref{sec:design}) trains the embedding table from scratch. What remains different is the hidden layers of the deep averaging network, the soft-logit targets, and the billion-parameter n-gram table designed for English. The frozen-embedding ablation, which trains only the linear head on top of potion, serves as an off-the-shelf Model2Vec baseline with a distilled head. The classifier trainer of the Model2Vec library was not run as a baseline. Its default configuration trains on hard labels and adds a 512-unit hidden layer. It also learns a weight per token. The ablations replace soft labels with hard labels and the linear head with a 512-unit hidden layer one at a time, and neither change differs significantly from the final recipe on the development or test set (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The two changes together and the learned token weights were not tested. A hidden layer also prevents folding the model into one table.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Statistical Tests}
\label{sec:protocol}
The main metric is macro-F1 over the labels present in the reference, as in the evaluation code of~\cite{kuzman2025llm}, because the label distribution is imbalanced. The evaluation also reports accuracy, macro-F1 per language averaged over languages so that large languages do not dominate, and agreement with the teacher on the fidelity set. On editorial labels, macro-F1 reads as agreement between the predictions and the categorization of the publisher.

95\% confidence intervals come from a percentile bootstrap with 1,000 resamples. On the test set, the resampling unit is the site, or the source dataset when no URL is available (site-cluster bootstrap~\cite{field2007bootstrapping}). On the development set, the unit is the document. Two models on the same documents are compared with a paired (cluster) bootstrap with 2,000 resamples for $\Delta$macro-F1~\cite{koehn2004significance} and an exact McNemar test for accuracy~\cite{mcnemar1947}, with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979} within each set. Each ablation variant uses five seeds, and the final recipe uses ten. Differences between variants are tested with Welch tests at the seed level, Holm-corrected. Whether a difference holds consistently across languages is tested with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-language macro-F1 and accuracy~\cite{dror2018hitchhiker}. Subgroup analyses cover label origin, region, the amount of distillation data of the language, site diversity, script, language, and label. Calibration is measured with the expected calibration error (ECE, 15 bins)~\cite{guo2017calibration}.

\subsection{Efficiency Protocol}
\label{sec:effprotocol}
The benchmark runs on CPU only, with the GPU hidden. Each model runs in a fresh process, and the thread count is pinned in every library (OMP, MKL, OpenBLAS, ONNX Runtime, \texttt{tokenizers}). Latency is measured with one article per call, as in a streaming setting (p50 and p95), and throughput with batches of 64, or 8 for transformers. Model RAM is the increase in resident set size after loading the model. The text sample is drawn at random from the unified test set and is the same for every model: 30 latency and 48 throughput documents for the teacher and the transformers, and 500 and 3,000 for the light models. All models are measured in one session on an idle machine. StaticKD reads up to 1,024 tokens per article while the teacher stops at 512, so the speed comparison does not favor StaticKD.

The machine is a laptop with an Intel Core Ultra~7 155H, 31~GB of RAM, and Windows~11. This CPU is hybrid, with 6 P-cores, 8 slower E-cores, and 2 low-power E-cores. Without pinning, the operating system moves threads to the slow cores. Each process is therefore pinned to physical P-cores (logical CPU 0 for one core; logical CPUs 0, 10, 12, and 14 for four cores), which approximates a small server with uniform cores.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Implementation Details}
\label{sec:design}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} lists all 72 StaticKD runs. Each ablation variant changes one factor of the final recipe: hard labels instead of soft labels; EMMediaTopic data only (4 languages, 10 epochs); 130 thousand documents without the extra CC-News data; random initialization with the same standard deviation as potion; frozen embeddings; no token dropout; a 512-unit multilayer perceptron (MLP) head; and $\tau\in\{0.5,\,2,\,4\}$. Four exploratory variants were also tested: bigram hashing ($2^{21}$ buckets), idf$^{0.5}$-weighted pooling, document weights from teacher confidence, and an additional cross-entropy term on the GPT-4o labels. One StaticKD run takes 5.2 minutes on average on the same GPU. The implementation uses Python~3.12, NumPy~2.5, PyTorch~2.6, transformers~5.17, \texttt{tokenizers}~0.23, ONNX Runtime~1.30, scikit-learn~1.9, and fastText~0.9.2. The seed, library versions, and training history of every run are stored with the model.

\subsection{Validity of the Editorial Labels}
\label{sec:res-validity}
Section and category labels record newsroom decisions, so the question is whether they agree with human labels in the same language.

All English cells of the test set are filled with human-labeled sources. As a comparison within the same language, a separate sample of 754 English CC-NEWS articles with JSON-LD section labels was therefore drawn from 306 sites outside the test set, at most 50 per label. The teacher reaches an accuracy of 0.646 on the section labels (macro-F1 0.617) and 0.559 on the MN-DS and MasakhaNEWS human labels (macro-F1 0.565). Accuracy on section labels is higher for 12 of the 17 labels (Wilcoxon $p$ 0.033), and the ranking of label difficulty correlates between the two label types ($\rho=0.63$, $p$ 0.007). The hard labels, \emph{society}, \emph{human interest}, and \emph{religion}, are hard under both.

French offers a second comparison inside the test set. The teacher reaches an accuracy of 0.716 on 208 articles with section labels and 0.500 on 200 human-labeled MasakhaNEWS articles.

A last check looks at each source category, restricted to languages covered by XLM-R pre-training. The median agreement of the teacher with the mapped label per category is 0.46 for MasakhaNEWS, 0.50 for MN-DS, 0.81 for L3Cube, and 0.87 for CC-NEWS sections. The lowest agreement occurs for source categories that are broader than their IPTC target. For example, only 0.45 of MasakhaNEWS \emph{politics} articles are predicted as \emph{politics}, with conflict (29\%) as the main alternative. Agreement is also low for labels whose boundaries are unclear even to human annotators, such as \emph{society} in MN-DS, in line with the low inter-annotator agreement reported for that label~\cite{kuzman2025llm}. Such mismatches reflect differences between the category scheme of a source and IPTC. Since every model is scored against the same labels, comparisons between models are unaffected, and absolute scores read as agreement with the editorial categorization.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
StaticKD keeps 91.1\% of the teacher macro-F1 on the unified test set and 92.6\% on the development set, at an inference cost two to three orders of magnitude lower (616$\times$ against ONNX int8 and 1,420$\times$ against PyTorch). This agrees with the finding that order-free representations are already competitive for topic classification~\cite{galke2022bow}. Names of institutions, sports terms, and medical vocabulary carry most of the topic signal. The remaining gap concentrates in two places. The first is labels whose vocabulary overlaps with other topics. The largest gaps to the teacher occur for environment ($-$0.17), religion ($-$0.15), and arts ($-$0.08). The hardest labels for StaticKD, given here with their F1, are society (0.16), disaster (0.29), human int. (0.32), and conflict (0.37). They are also among the hardest for the teacher. \emph{Society} and \emph{human interest} are known to be difficult for human annotators as well~\cite{kuzman2025llm}. The second is languages that are underrepresented in the distillation data. StaticKD reaches 97.5\% of the teacher score in languages with sufficient distillation data but only 72.3\% in languages without any.

With the same distillation data, MiniLM-L6 closes part of this gap (StaticKD $-$ MiniLM $-$0.021, $p$ 0.004), whereas DistilmBERT does not (+0.010, $p$ 0.800). Both cost one to two orders of magnitude more than StaticKD. MiniLM comes from a distillation of XLM-R-large and DistilmBERT from mBERT, which suggests that the quality of the initial multilingual representation matters more than the number of layers.

The ablations show where the knowledge of the student comes from (RQ3). The largest source is the multilingual distillation data labeled by the teacher. Without it the test macro-F1 drops by $-$0.051, consistent with Sparse Distillation~\cite{ye2022sparse}, which also relies on a large transfer set. The cross-lingually aligned initialization comes next, and the compact variant gives the most direct evidence for it. In Indian languages that hardly occur in the distillation data (for example bn, pa, or), most tokens are never updated during training and keep their initial potion embeddings. StaticKD still classifies these languages with a mean accuracy of 0.624, yet the accuracy falls to 0.013 once the tokenizer pruning removes those tokens. Transfer to languages without data is therefore carried by embedding rows that were never trained but live in the same space as the trained rows, so the linear head learned on data-rich languages applies to them too.

The frozen-embedding ablation shows the other side of the same mechanism. Fine-tuning moves the embeddings of tokens from languages in the distillation data and improves in-distribution fit. Freezing them lowers the development score by $-$0.035. Tokens of other languages stay in the original potion space, where the head that adapted to the moved space fits them less well. In languages with no or little distillation data, frozen embeddings are better by +0.037 and +0.059. This is representation drift caused by fine-tuning part of the vocabulary. The final recipe keeps fine-tuning because the recipe was selected on a development set in the training languages. For deployments that target languages without distillation data, frozen embeddings or a regularizer that keeps the embeddings close to potion are the safer choice, and both still fold into a table.

Soft labels, by contrast, add little or nothing. Hard teacher labels do not differ significantly from soft labels at $\tau=1$ (development $-$0.006, test +0.005). Raising the temperature to $\tau=2$ lowers the held-out agreement without a significant change on the test set, and $\tau=4$ lowers every metric. This pattern fits the capacity-gap literature~\cite{zhang2025law,wang2026rckd}. A plausible explanation is that one linear layer over averaged embeddings cannot represent the inter-class structure in the tail of the teacher distribution (dark knowledge)~\cite{zhao2022dkd}. A sharp distribution ($\tau=1$) or hard labels give an attainable target, whereas a high $\tau$ asks the student to imitate a distribution shape beyond its capacity. For very-low-capacity students, effort is better spent on the coverage of the transfer set (languages, sites, topics) and on aligned initialization than on shaping the soft labels.

Per-language performance is driven mainly by how well a language is represented in the distillation and pre-training data, not by its script (Kruskal--Wallis $p$ 0.065). The weakest languages are African languages outside XLM-R pre-training that are also missing from the distillation data (ig, lg, ln, pcm, rn, sn, ti, yo). The teacher still reaches an accuracy of 0.643 on them, perhaps thanks to subwords shared with other languages and to sentence context, while StaticKD reaches only 0.407. The cheapest fix needs no change of architecture. News in these languages can simply be added to the transfer set and labeled by the teacher. The data ablation and the correlation between agreement and the amount of data ($\rho=0.31$) support this step.

Some variants move agreement with the teacher and accuracy on editorial labels in opposite directions. Frozen embeddings lower the held-out agreement ($-$0.020) but raise the test macro-F1 (+0.014). Cutting the data to 130 thousand documents lowers the agreement ($-$0.009) without a significant change on the test set ($-$0.002). In line with~\cite{stanton2021kd}, students should be selected with labels that do not come from the teacher and with test data outside the training distribution, not with fidelity alone.

Test scores should be read according to the nature of the labels. Two thirds of the labels are editorial topics, the place where the newsroom put an article, translated to IPTC. An article about a criminal case involving an athlete can rightly sit in a sports section while a model labels it \emph{crime}. Both answers are plausible under IPTC, which allows several topics per article. Absolute scores on editorial labels thus measure agreement with the categorization of publishers. Comparisons between models remain fair because all models are scored against the same labels, and the model ranking does not change on the subset with capped site counts.

Compared with Sparse Distillation~\cite{ye2022sparse}, StaticKD replaces an n-gram table trained from scratch with pre-trained multilingual embeddings folded into 17 logits per token. The model becomes far smaller and transfers to languages that are missing from the transfer set. Compared with LoRA and PEFT~\cite{hu2022lora,razuvayevskaya2024peft}, StaticKD lowers the cost of inference rather than of training. Inference is the recurring cost in a news pipeline, since every incoming article is classified, and one million articles per day need 15.1 cores with the ONNX int8 teacher against 0.02 with StaticKD (Section~\ref{sec:res-eff}). Int8 quantization of the teacher only gives a constant-factor speed-up.

For a real-time pipeline on servers without a GPU, StaticKD processes one million articles per day with 0.02 cores, a 34~MB file, and \texttt{numpy} and \texttt{tokenizers} as its only dependencies. Its probabilities are better calibrated than those of the teacher (test ECE 0.139 against 0.245), which makes a cascade possible. With $\theta=0.8$, the cascade accuracy (0.703) equals that of the teacher alone (0.699) at 1.9 articles per second per core, against 0.77 for the teacher alone. This mode suits batch jobs that need teacher-level accuracy. The compact variant lowers RAM but is only advisable when all target languages are covered by the distillation data.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Of the test articles, 47.5\% carry JSON-LD section labels and 20.4\% publisher categories. These labels are editorial topics. Section conventions differ between publishers, and one section reflects one editorial view of an article that may cover several topics. The mapping table is conservative, validated against the IPTC tree, and reviewed by the first author, but the articles were not re-annotated by humans. Absolute scores on these labels therefore measure agreement with the categorization of publishers, especially for labels with unclear boundaries (\emph{society}, \emph{human interest}, \emph{lifestyle}).

MasakhaNEWS labels were assigned by human annotators under their own scheme and then mapped to IPTC. Only MN-DS is annotated directly with IPTC topics, and it is English only. The multilingual IPTC test set of Kuzman and Ljube\v{s}i\'c~\cite{kuzman2025llm} is not public, so it was not available for this study.

Label coverage per language is uneven, with a median of 7 labels per language, and the African languages cover only four or five broad topics. Macro-F1 is computed over the labels present, so per-language scores are not directly comparable between languages with different label coverage. Publisher diversity is also limited for some languages: 27 languages come from practically one publisher, namely BBC/VOA for the African languages, L3Cube for the Indian languages, and a single site for several CC-NEWS languages. Claims for these languages are limited to those publishers, and the site-cluster bootstrap widens their confidence intervals accordingly.

The teacher bounds the student from above, and its errors and biases are copied. The fidelity set measures similarity to the teacher, not correctness.

The hyperparameters of fastText and TF-IDF + SVM were set once without a search, whereas the StaticKD recipe was selected from 72 runs on the development set. This unequal tuning budget may widen the accuracy gap to these two baselines. The classifier trainer of the Model2Vec library was not run as a baseline, and only its components were tested separately as ablations. MiniLM-L6 and DistilmBERT were trained and evaluated with at most 256 tokens and therefore see less than half of most test articles, so their accuracy may be underestimated.

The benchmark ran on one machine in one session, with 30 latency documents for the teacher and the transformers. Absolute numbers on a server will differ and the latency sample of the heavy models is small, but ratios between models measured under the same protocol remain informative.

StaticKD is much less accurate on African languages that are absent from both the distillation data and pre-training (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Used as a topic filter, it could misclassify or drop news in these languages more often. Before deployment, accuracy should be monitored per language, and low-confidence articles should be routed to the teacher through the cascade mode.

The study covers only top-level IPTC labels and single-label classification. Deeper levels and multi-topic articles remain untested.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study measured the CPU cost of the open multilingual IPTC classifier and showed that it can be cut by two to three orders of magnitude while keeping most of its accuracy. StaticKD distills the XLM-R-large teacher into a table of 17 logits per token, initialized from multilingual static embeddings and trained on 285,999 teacher-labeled articles in 69 languages. On a unified test set of 12,744 articles in 47 languages, with labels that do not come from the teacher, StaticKD reaches a macro-F1 of 0.545, or 91.1\% of the teacher. It processes 473 articles per second per CPU core, about 616 times the speed of the ONNX int8 teacher, with a 34~MB model that needs no deep learning library.

The accuracy of this very-low-capacity student comes mainly from the multilingual transfer set and from cross-lingually aligned embeddings. Soft labels are no better than hard labels, $\tau=4$ lowers every metric, and an ensemble of seeds folds into one table at no cost. Fine-tuning the embeddings raises accuracy in languages present in the distillation data but lowers it in languages without distillation data, because transfer to those languages is carried by pre-trained embeddings that are never updated. The test set, with editorial labels mapped onto the IPTC tree, contamination control, and site-cluster bootstrap, can be reused to evaluate other IPTC classifiers.

Several directions follow from these results. The transfer set should be extended to the weak languages, especially the African languages outside XLM-R pre-training. A fine-tuning regularizer that keeps embeddings close to the pre-trained space could give in-distribution accuracy and cross-lingual transfer at the same time. Tokenization takes 98\% of the inference time, so a faster tokenizer, for instance a direct word-to-table lookup, is the next source of speed. Any pruning of the vocabulary has to keep cross-lingual tokens. Cheap non-linear heads with quantized embeddings, hierarchical classification into IPTC level 2, multi-label classification, and human IPTC annotation of a multilingual sample of the test set remain open.
